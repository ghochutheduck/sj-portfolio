>
> **On "preferred" versus "open to":** *"Open to remote roles"* is the ambiguous phrasing — it confirms you'd take remote and leaves an on-site employer unable to tell whether you're available at all. *"Remote preferred"* can only be read one way, because preferring something implies an alternative you'd still accept. It states the preference and keeps every door open, in one word.
>
> **The one cost of naming both:** a recruiter hiring full-time reads a candidate advertising freelance alongside it as marginally less committed to the role. It's a small effect and you've weighed it — being visibly available for project work matters to you, and it's true. Worth knowing it's the trade you're making.
>
> Also be aware some traditional employers read "remote preferred" from a recent graduate as someone who'll be harder to mentor. Your answer is one screen down: you run a business with paying members entirely on your own. Strongest possible counter-argument, already on the page, so the hero doesn't need to make it.

## Why it exists

I have made things with my hands for as long as I can remember. Birthdays, Christmas, Mother's Day. I'd make something: a letter, a small piece of art, anything I could give someone to make them happy. I've never stopped picking up new ways to do it. Quilling, clay, knitting, calligraphy, linocut, collage, sewing, pattern making. If it can be made by hand, I've probably tried it.

That's why I went to design school. Four years of putting creativity on a deadline, on a screen, slowly wore down the thing that sent me there. By the end I had stopped trusting my own work.

What brought it back was small. My cousin gave me a set of acrylic markers, and I started making things again, for the pleasure of making them. The pressure to be perfect lifted, and I wanted to give things to people again.

BoopShaillyBoop is that impulse, turned into something I can send.

## The idea

Everything arrives instantly now, and most of it means nothing.

A physical envelope carries proof that someone sat down, spent their time, and made something slowly, by hand, for you. It's delayed gratification in a world that has stopped asking anyone to wait. That's the whole product.

## Decisions

**The first batch is capped at 30.**
Partly to see how people actually respond before scaling anything. Mostly because I know myself. If it got big too fast, the old pressure to be perfect would come straight back, and that's the thing I built this to get away from. I want the beginning to be small, personal, and good.

**Every envelope in a batch is identical.**
Everyone gets the same thing in the same week, and that shared experience is what turns a list of subscribers into a club. Members start recognising each other's work. They have something in common, because the same envelope landed on all of their doormats.

It's also why the work stays exclusive. I will never sell the mail club prints anywhere else. They exist only inside the club, and they belong only to the members who were there that month.

The one thing I do individually, and always will, no matter how big this gets: every letter is signed by hand.

**Everything is designed to weigh under 50g.**
Constraints are great decision makers. The postal weight limit sets the shipping cost, the shipping cost sets the subscription price, and the price decides who can afford to join. Art shouldn't be inaccessible, and neither should mine, since the entire point of it is bringing people joy. The weight limit is what keeps this open to anyone.

Working inside it made me learn paper properly: weights, stocks, what survives Indian post. I print with local printers in Patna, my hometown, because being there in person is the only way I get it exactly right.

**One member's artwork goes into everyone's envelope.**
Members send in work by the 21st, and one piece gets printed into that month's mail for everybody, with the artist's details alongside it.

This one is personal. There was a stretch in college where I couldn't make anything at all, and I had lost my gut instinct for what was good. I wanted a place where art finds an audience even when the person who made it doubts it. Members get to know each other as artists, and they make things for the club knowing perfection was never the price of entry.

## What I built

I don't write code from scratch. I work with AI tools, and I know enough to specify what I need, test it, and debug it when it breaks.

**The site.** Hand built in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, dependency free, deployed from GitHub. Eight pages sharing one stylesheet and one script, with a documented design system and a decision log recording what I tried and what I rejected.

**Payments.** Live Razorpay checkout for one time and subscription plans.

**The member form.** A multi step "slam book" with auto saving drafts that writes straight into a Google Sheet, which doubles as the member database and generates my whole shipping list at the click of one button.

**Boop Ledger.** A private accounting app for the club. Subscription cash and subscription revenue are different things: when someone pays ₹4,800 for a year, ₹400 of it is earned this month and the remaining ₹4,400 is still owed in envelopes. I studied accounting in school and learned early that it doesn't matter how small a thing starts, you build it so it survives getting bigger. So the club runs on double entry bookkeeping, which removes the room for error and gives it a foundation that holds at any size. It lives on my phone, logs an expense in ten seconds, and it will still be right when the club is ten times bigger.

## What went wrong

I wanted the payment buttons to match the site exactly, and Razorpay's subscription buttons wouldn't allow it. So I built dedicated checkout pages that sit between the site and the gateway, keeping the handoff seamless.

Then a harder problem. People get cold feet about automatic recurring payments, and I wanted everyone to see their first envelope before committing to a year of them. So recurring became a checkbox. Tick it and never think about paying again, or leave it and pay each time. The choice sits with the member.

## What surprised me

I built this for people my own age, and I was wrong about that. The largest group signing up has been people between 40 and 60: people who grew up writing letters, waiting for replies, and knowing the weight of a stamped envelope. They understood the product immediately, because they had already lived it.

It's too early to say what I do about that. One envelope has shipped. I've noticed the pattern and I'm not going to pretend I've drawn the right conclusion from it yet, but it's the kind of thing that changes who you make each month's theme for, and I expect it will.

> Changed from the original, which said the discovery "changed how I talk about the club and who I make each month's theme for." You told me it's too early to say, so the page says that. Noticing a pattern and refusing to over-read it after one issue is a stronger signal to a hiring manager than a tidy conclusion would be — it shows you know the difference between a data point and a trend.

**The three month plan is also the one people are choosing.** I had assumed most would test the water with a single envelope, but three months turns out to be the point where the price feels fair and the commitment feels small. It tells me people are joining for the rhythm of it rather than the novelty, which is exactly what a club needs.

## What working looks like

**Where it is:** one envelope shipped, 31 members, the second in production.

**Where it's going:** a hundred members in a year, and a club where people look forward to each other's art as much as mine.

And, honestly, staying in love with making things, and getting to hand that feeling to other people. If it keeps doing that, it's working.
